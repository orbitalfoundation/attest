technical passkeys, delegation, AT Protocol, threat model

Identity: a passkey and an AT Protocol account

Sign-up does two things. It registers a passkey by WebAuthn with residentKey: required and ES256 as the only accepted algorithm, so the passkey is a P-256 key held by the person's device. And it creates an AT Protocol account on our data server (PDS): a did:plc in the public PLC directory, a repository, and a handle <name>.attest.monster bound to the DID by a DNS TXT record at _atproto.<handle>. The account's password is a random server-side secret nobody sees; the passkey is the person's key. We store the credential id, the passkey's public key, its signature counter, the handle and the DID. There is no email and no password the person uses.

The passkey's public key is also expressed as a did:key (multicodec prefix for P-256, 0x1200, then the 33-byte compressed point, base58btc with a z prefix; these start did:key:zDna). The account's identity is the did:plc; the passkey is what controls the account's delegations. A passkey cannot itself be the PLC rotation key, because AT Protocol rotation keys sign raw bytes and WebAuthn signs a challenge structure, so the rotation key for accounts made here is held by the service and backed up off the server.

Delegation to a device key

WebAuthn signatures are bound to the relying party's origin and each one prompts the user. A site on another origin cannot ask your passkey to sign, and a prompt per upvote is unusable. So the passkey signs a delegation instead.

delegation {v:1, type:"delegation", root, device, devKey:{x,y}, origin, from, until}
  • device is a P-256 key the integrating page generated with WebCrypto, private half non-extractable, stored in that origin's IndexedDB. It never leaves the browser and never reaches us in private form. devKey is its public JWK and device must equal didFromJwk(devKey).
  • origin is the bare origin of the page that will use the delegation. The server refuses a record whose socket handshake origin differs from it. One site cannot act as you on another; each site is its own arena, in Friedman and Resnick's sense.
  • from must be within ten minutes of server time; until is at most 90 days later. The login page issues 30.

The delegation's id is the SHA-256 of its canonical JSON. That id, as bytes, is the WebAuthn challenge; the passkey's assertion over it is the signature on the delegation. Sign-in therefore is the act of delegating, and a returning user sees one prompt. The assertion (authenticatorData, clientDataJSON, signature) is stored with the delegation and appended to the log, so anyone with the root's public key can verify the delegation later, offline. The signature counter is updated and monotonic.

On a page of another origin, the login page runs in a popup on our origin (so the passkey works), receives the page's device DID and public JWK in the query string, and posts the finished session back with postMessage to the origin in the delegation. On our own origin it is a redirect.

Records in repositories

A record is an AT Protocol record in the author's repository, in one of our lexicons, monster.attest.{vote, comment, statement, vouch, claim, verification}, published at _lexicon.attest.monster. Each carries the author's inline signature in the shared app.certified.signature.defs#inline shape, as in badge.blue and Hypercerts: the device key signs the 36-byte CIDv1 (DAG-CBOR, SHA-256) of the record with its signatures removed and a $sig object {$type, key, repository} inserted. ECDSA P-256, raw r||s, low-S. Because the repository DID is inside what is signed, a signature cannot be replayed into another repository.

The client builds and signs the record; the service checks the signature against the delegated device key and the repository DID, checks the delegation is live and was issued for the calling origin, validates the record against the lexicon, and writes it into the repository through the PDS. Votes, vouches and claims use a record key derived from the subject, so there is one per author per subject; comments and statements use time-ordered keys. Retraction deletes the record, authorised by a device-key signature over {type:"retract", uri, at}.

The service's index (SQLite) is derived from the repositories. It follows the PDS's subscribeRepos firehose: a record in our collections written by any client is verified against its repository and indexed, an unsigned one is ignored, a deletion retracts, and an account's deletion or deactivation removes its records from all counts. Delegations, revocations and passkey changes, which are not repository records, are kept in the index's own log with their passkey assertions.

Identities proven on Bluesky are linked, and the trust they have already declared on AT Protocol is imported as edges: Tangled vouches and denouncements (sh.tangled.graph.vouch, subject in the record key), Tangled follows and Bluesky follows. Each is kept as its own kind so a scorer can weigh them differently. Imports refresh every six hours.

Stolen delegation without the device keyUseless: the device private key is non-extractable and never transmitted.
Replay of a recordThe signed input includes the repository DID, so a record cannot be replayed into another repository; within one, votes, vouches and claims have subject-derived keys, so a duplicate is a no-op.

The service stopsIdentities are in the public PLC directory; the rotation key and repository data are backed up off the server; records are copied by relays. The service can move to another host.
Reads reveal what you look atTrue for a site's own reads through us. The planned browser client scores from a copy of the records or asks by hash prefix.

Root actions

Sign-in is one instance of a general shape: the passkey signs an action by signing a WebAuthn assertion whose challenge is the action's SHA-256. The others are revoke (a delegation), add-key and remove-key (passkeys on the account). Each is kept in the index's log with its assertion, so account history can be replayed and checked. The service offers only the account's own passkeys in the browser prompt, and never the one being removed.

Proof of control and the service key

A claim record says a key controls a handle or URL. The token attest-proof:<claim cid> goes where only the controller can put it: the page itself or /.well-known/attest.txt for a website, a TXT record at _attest.<domain>, a public gist, a Bluesky bio. The service fetches, and if it finds the token it writes a verification record into its own repository (attest.attest.monster, DID at /service), signed inline by its own P-256 key, with the evidence URL. Any other account may verify a claim by writing its own verification; each counts as far as its author is trusted. A verified Bluesky claim also links that identity for the graph read-in above.

Canonical JSON

Used only for delegations, revocations and retraction authorisations, which are not repository records: object keys sorted at every level, arrays in order, undefined dropped, no whitespace. Repository records are hashed as DAG-CBOR, as AT Protocol specifies.
